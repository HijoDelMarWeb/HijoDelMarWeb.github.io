What is an EPIRB and Why Does Your Vessel Need One?

The beacon that can be the difference between being rescued and disappearing at sea.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso Peirano  ·  June 2026  ·  8 min read

Throughout my years at sea, I've seen two types of sailors: those who carry an EPIRB and those who don't. The first sleep better. The second, in the worst case, don't sleep anywhere again.

I don't say this to frighten anyone. I say it because the EPIRB is one of the most important safety pieces of equipment for navigation, and at the same time one of the least understood. Many people don't know exactly what it does, when it activates or how to register it. This guide exists to clear all that up.

What exactly is an EPIRB?

EPIRB stands for Emergency Position-Indicating Radio Beacon. It's an electronic device that, when activated in an emergency, transmits a distress signal to the international satellite system COSPAS-SARSAT.

That system is composed of low-orbit and geostationary satellites that receive the signal and retransmit it to ground stations. Those stations calculate the exact position of the beacon and alert the search and rescue coordination center (SAR) of the corresponding country — in our case, the Chilean Navy.

The result: within minutes or hours, rescue teams know where you are with meter-level precision.

"The EPIRB doesn't prevent accidents. But it can be the reason rescue arrives in time."

How does it work in practice?

There are two ways to activate an EPIRB: manually, when you press the activation button yourself, and automatically, when the device comes into contact with water. This second mode has saved the most lives — because in many maritime emergencies the crew has no time to activate anything.

Modern Category 1 EPIRBs have a hydrostatic release mechanism: if the vessel sinks, the device releases itself, floats, and activates automatically on contact with water. This works even if no one on board is conscious.

Types of EPIRB
  • Category 1: Activates automatically on water contact (hydrostatic release). Most recommended for offshore navigation and the southern canals
  • Category 2: Manual activation only. Cheaper, but less safe — if you lose consciousness, it won't transmit
  • PLB (Personal Locator Beacon): Personal version, smaller, for each individual crew member. Does not replace the vessel EPIRB

Is the EPIRB mandatory in Chile?

The short answer is: it depends on the navigation category. The practical answer is: if you sail offshore or in remote areas, yes.

The Chilean Maritime Authority (Directemar) establishes equipment requirements by navigation category. For oceanic navigation, major coastal routes and remote areas like the southern canals, the EPIRB is a mandatory requirement for obtaining departure clearance. For sports vessels in coastal navigation, requirements vary by operating area.

My advice: don't sail without one, regardless of whether it's required. The cost of an EPIRB is minimal compared to the value of a life.

Common mistake

Many sailors buy an EPIRB but don't register it. An unregistered EPIRB is nearly useless: when it transmits, rescue teams have no information about the vessel or who is on board. Registration is mandatory and free.

How to register your EPIRB in Chile

Registration is done with the Chilean Navy through the nearest Harbour Master's office, or online through the Directemar system. You need the EPIRB's identification number (the 15-digit HEX code printed on the device), your vessel details and your personal contact information.

This registration means that when your EPIRB transmits, rescuers immediately know which vessel the signal corresponds to, how many people are typically on board, and who to call ashore. This information can significantly accelerate the rescue.

EPIRB maintenance
  • Battery: 5-year service life — must be replaced even if the device has never been used
  • Annual check: inspect the device's status indicator
  • Activation test: you can do a short test during the first 5 minutes of the first hour of each month (international protocol) — but notify the Navy beforehand
  • Update the registration if you change vessel or contact details
  • Storage: in an accessible location from deck, not in a locked drawer below

What happens if it activates accidentally?

Accidental activations are more common than people realize and have serious consequences. They can mobilize rescue resources unnecessarily, put other sailors at risk and generate significant costs. If your EPIRB activates by accident, switch it off immediately and notify the Navy or the nearest coast guard.

EPIRB vs PLB: do I need both?

This is a question I get often. The answer is that they are not the same and do not replace each other.

The vessel EPIRB is designed to identify and locate the vessel in distress. The PLB is a personal device that each crew member can carry on them — especially useful if someone falls overboard and drifts away from the vessel. In that case, the vessel's EPIRB is useless for that person.

For navigation in the southern canals or high-exposure areas, I recommend both: Category 1 EPIRB on the vessel, and one PLB per crew member.
