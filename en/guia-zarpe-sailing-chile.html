Zarpe in Chile: How to Obtain Your Sailing Clearance

Sailing without a zarpe in Chilean waters is a serious offence. Here is what you need, how to get it, and what happens if you do not.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso · July 2026 · 9 min read

The zarpe is one of those pieces of paperwork many sailors see as bureaucracy, and in practice it's much more than that. In my years as a Captain I've seen incidents where the difference between a successful search and a tragedy was precisely whether the zarpe had been filed correctly. When the Navy knows you left, where you were headed, and how many were on board, rescue begins immediately.

What the Zarpe Is and Why It Exists

The zarpe is the departure clearance issued by Chile's Maritime Authority — Directemar, through the local Port Captain's offices — before you sail. It isn't a discretionary permit: it's a registration system that documents the vessel, crew, destination and safety equipment.

The reasoning behind the zarpe is operational, not bureaucratic: if an emergency occurs and someone triggers the rescue system, the Navy knows exactly what to look for, where to look, and how many people to rescue. Without that record, the search starts from zero.

Types of Zarpe in Chile

Local zarpe

For sailing within a bay, roadstead or protected zone, without staying overnight away from the home port. It's the simplest and can be obtained the same day. Equipment requirements are lighter, but the vessel must be registered with Directemar and the skipper must hold the relevant licence.

Cabotaje (coastal) zarpe

For sailing between Chilean ports, with the possibility of staying overnight aboard and moving further from the coast. Requires more complete documentation: current registration certificate, vessel insurance in some cases, a full crew roll, and safety equipment matching the vessel's authorised navigation category.

Altura (offshore) zarpe

For ocean or international navigation. Involves a more detailed inspection of safety equipment (EPIRB, life raft, VHF, radar) and may require the vessel to hold an up-to-date technical certificate. To leave Chile for an international destination, an international zarpe with Customs clearance is required in addition to the Port Captain's authorisation.

ZARPEX — online zarpe: Directemar runs an online system for managing zarpes for recreational vessels registered in the small-craft registry. It lets you process local and simple cabotaje zarpes without visiting the Port Captain's office in person. It requires prior registration in the system and the vessel's registration to be up to date. Available on Directemar's website.

Documents Needed for the Zarpe

  • Current registration certificate: The vessel's registration with Directemar, showing the authorised navigation category
  • Crew roll: Full name and national ID (RUT) or passport number for each person aboard
  • Previous zarpe returned: If applicable — the zarpe from your last outing, returned to the Port Captain's office on your return
  • Skipper's qualification: Current nautical licence or navigation certificate matching the zarpe category
  • Safety equipment: As required by the zarpe category: life jackets, EPIRB, VHF, life raft, flares and whatever else applies to the planned voyage
The zarpe doesn't return itself

When you return to your home port, you must return the zarpe to the relevant Port Captain's office. If you don't, Directemar assumes you're still on the water and may initiate a search procedure, which creates costs and complications for you.

Validity and Limitations of the Zarpe

A local zarpe is typically valid for 12 to 24 hours. A cabotaje zarpe's validity depends on the declared itinerary. If your outing runs longer than planned, you must notify the nearest Port Captain's office and request an extension. Deviating from the declared itinerary without notice can also trigger search alerts.

The navigation category authorised on your vessel's registration limits the type of zarpe you can request. A vessel registered for "bay" navigation cannot obtain a cabotaje zarpe even if the skipper is licensed for it. The vessel sets the limit, not just the skipper.
