How to Plan a Sailing Voyage

Everything you need to consider before casting off — from the nautical chart to the contingency plan.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso Peirano · June 2026 · 12 min read

In 30 years at sea I've seen two kinds of crews arrive in an unfamiliar port: those who planned well and those who got there by luck. The difference is almost never the boat or the experience. It's the planning.

A sailing voyage is not something you improvise — at least not if you want it to go well and everyone to come back safely. And planning doesn't mean eliminating the adventure of sailing. It means knowing the risks before you face them, not in the middle of them.

This guide walks through everything you need to consider before casting off: from how to study the route to what documents to carry on board. It applies equally to a weekend coastal passage and a multi-week ocean voyage.

«Good planning doesn't eliminate the adventure. It means knowing the risks before the sea presents them to you.»

1. Define what type of voyage you're making

Before pulling out charts or checking the forecast, you need to be clear about what kind of sailing you're planning. A two-day coastal passage is very different from a three-week ocean crossing. Each has different requirements for equipment, crew, documentation and weather planning.

Voyage types
Coastal, offshore or ocean

Coastal: you navigate close to land, with the ability to enter harbour within hours if the weather deteriorates. Lower risk, but not without danger: shorelines have rocks, currents and tides that don't appear in a general forecast.

Offshore: you move away from the coast without crossing oceans. You may spend nights at sea with no land in sight. Requires organised night watches, longer-range communications and a crew capable of handling the boat without immediate assistance.

Ocean: crossing oceans or large distances between continents or archipelagos. Requires exhaustive preparation, a boat fit for severe conditions, and complete self-sufficiency for weeks.

Practical rule: if at any point on the voyage you could be more than 4 hours from a safe harbour, it's no longer coastal sailing. Adjust your preparation accordingly.

2. Study the route in detail

Once you know what you're doing, the next step is knowing the terrain. At sea, the terrain changes constantly: tides, currents, local winds, seabed and navigation restrictions. You need to work from updated nautical charts — not tourist maps or general-purpose apps.

Nautical charts

In Chile, the official authority is the Servicio Hidrográfico y Oceanográfico de la Armada (SHOA). Their charts are the valid reference for Chilean navigation and are available in paper or digital format. For international waters, British Admiralty charts (UKHO) and Electronic Navigational Charts (ENCs) compatible with most modern plotters are widely used.

When studying the chart, identify: shallow water zones and submerged rocks; currents and counter-currents in narrow passages; buoys, lighthouses and marks that will guide your navigation; and ports or anchorages for shelter at each stage.

Distances, times and fuel

Calculate the total distance in nautical miles and divide the voyage into manageable daily stages. For a cruising sailboat, a typical stage is 80 to 120 miles in normal conditions, assuming 6 to 8 knots average speed. Don't plan to the limit of your capacity: always leave room for bad weather, flat calms or mechanical problems.

If your boat has an auxiliary engine, estimate the fuel you'll need for windless stages and harbour manoeuvres. Always carry a 20% fuel reserve.

What to identify on the chart before departure
  • Safe anchorages and shelter ports at each stage
  • Minimum depths in passages you'll navigate (with your draught plus tidal margin)
  • Restricted or prohibited areas (military, fishing, diving)
  • Channel marks and buoyage in ports
  • Rocks and shoals identified away from the visible coastline
  • Names and coordinates of passages and channels

3. Analyse the weather and meteorological conditions

Weather is the element that changes a voyage more than anything else. In Chile, where the sea can go from flat calm to 35 knots within a few hours — especially in the southern channels — meteorological analysis is not optional. It is a central part of planning.

Reliable information sources

For navigation in Chilean waters, the Armada Meteorological Service issues wind and wave forecasts for specific zones. Globally, Windy, PredictWind, PasaGrib and the GFS/ECMWF models are widely used by ocean sailors. No app replaces the navigator's judgment, but all are useful tools if you know how to read them.

Weather windows

A weather window is a period of favourable conditions for departure. On offshore and ocean passages, identifying a good window is as important as any other part of the plan. It's always better to wait three days in port than to leave with a front approaching.

Study the climate pattern of the area and the time of year. In central Chile, south and southwesterly winds dominate in summer. In the channels, willywaws — violent, sudden katabatic gusts — can appear without any meteorological warning. This is not something you learn from an app: it's something you learn from sailing those waters, or from asking someone who has.

«In 30 years at sea I learned that weather never lies. The one who lies is the navigator who decides to ignore it.»

4. Check the condition of your vessel

Before any significant voyage, the boat needs a systematic inspection. Not a visual check from the deck: a real inspection with checklists, getting your hands into the places that matter.

Hull and underwater gear

Check the condition of the hull, keel, rudder and centreboard if applicable. If you haven't hauled the boat out of the water in the past year, you don't know what state the underwater section is in. Marine growth increases drag and fuel consumption — but more importantly, it can hide damage or advanced electrolysis.

Rig and sails

Check stays, shrouds and the running rigging of halyards and sheets. Look for signs of wear at cable terminals and friction points. A failed sail at sea is a manageable problem; a broken stay is another matter. Sails should be inspected on shore in good light, looking for tears, worn patches at reinforcements and failing stitching.

Engine and systems

The engine is your life insurance when the wind disappears or when you need to manoeuvre in a tight harbour. Change the oil, filters and raw-water impellers before the voyage. Check the alternator belt, battery condition and cooling system. Carry basic spares on board: fuel filters, a spare impeller, belts, fuses and anodes.

Inspection checklist · Vessel
Before casting off, verify:

Hull with no visible cracks, keel and rudder in good condition, seacocks on all hull fittings operational, bilge pump working, engine oil and cooling in order, batteries charged and in good condition, electrical system with no shorts or loose wiring, standing rigging inspected with no advanced wear, sails in good condition with no failing seams.

Practical rule: if you're in doubt about something, fix it before you leave. There are no chandleries at sea.

5. Safety equipment

This is the area most sailors treat superficially — until they actually need it. Safety equipment is not a formality for passing an inspection: it is what protects lives when something goes wrong.

Minimum equipment for an offshore passage

A certified lifejacket for each person, with whistle, light and integrated or compatible harness. For offshore and ocean sailing, automatic inflatable lifejackets are the standard. The safest lifejacket is the one the crew member will actually wear — so make sure everyone puts theirs on before they need it.

A liferaft appropriate for the number of people on board and the type of sailing. Liferafts have periodic service dates — check that yours is current. Many sailors have a liferaft on board but have never opened one, don't know how it works and don't know where the painter is.

An EPIRB (Emergency Position Indicating Radio Beacon) registered and with a valid battery. The EPIRB transmits your position to rescue satellites when activated — either manually or automatically on contact with water. It is the difference between being rescued in hours and not being found at all.

Marine VHF, fixed and handheld, always on channel 16 (the international calling and distress channel). VHF has a range of 20 to 30 nautical miles in normal conditions — enough to contact coastguard or nearby vessels in an emergency.

Recommended safety equipment for offshore sailing
  • Certified lifejackets for every crew member
  • Harnesses and jacklines for night watches
  • Lifebuoy with light and recovery line
  • Liferaft with current service certificate
  • Registered EPIRB with valid battery
  • Fixed VHF (with DSC) and handheld VHF backup
  • Personal PLB for each crew member (recommended for ocean sailing)
  • Flares (parachute and handheld), in date and accessible
  • Fire extinguisher and first aid kit
  • Manual emergency bilge pump

6. Organise the crew

On a voyage lasting more than one day, the crew is as important as the boat. Fatigue is one of the main factors in errors at sea. A single sailor cannot handle a sailing boat in open ocean for days without making serious mistakes. Watches must be organised.

Watch system

The most common system divides the day into 3 or 4-hour watches, with two people per watch if numbers allow. Every crew member must know: when their watch is, what to do if the wind changes, how to use the VHF, where the emergency equipment is and how to alert the rest of the crew if something goes wrong.

Define before departure who is skipper — the person with final authority on safety decisions. Under pressure, the chain of command cannot be debated: someone must be able to say "we stop" and have everyone comply.

Experience level

Be honest about your crew's real experience. Someone having done inshore racing doesn't mean they're ready for a night watch offshore in 3-metre seas. Every person on board must genuinely be able to handle the boat — not just sit in the cockpit in light air.

7. Documentation and official departure

In Chile, every vessel must have its documentation in order and complete the official departure (zarpe) at the relevant Port Captain's office before putting to sea. This is not empty bureaucracy: in an emergency, the maritime authorities know exactly where you're going, when you expect to arrive and how many people are on board.

Basic documents on board (Chile)
  • Zarpe (departure clearance) from the Port Captain's office
  • Current vessel registration (DIRECTEMAR)
  • Safety and tonnage certificate
  • Skipper's navigation licence (if required for vessel type and area)
  • Crew list with personal data and ID numbers
  • Engine documents and insurance if applicable
  • Current liferaft service certificate

For international sailing, add passports, outward clearance from the country, customs clearance if carrying goods, and the vessel's international registration documents. Each country has its own requirements for maritime entry and exit.

8. Design a contingency plan

A contingency plan is not a sign of pessimism: it is a sign of nautical maturity. Things go wrong at sea. The engine fails, the anchor drags, water comes in, a crew member is injured, the wind arrives stronger than forecast. The navigator who has a plan for those scenarios makes better decisions under pressure.

For each leg of your voyage, identify: the nearest port or anchorage for shelter, the entry manoeuvre for that port (especially at night or in bad weather), and the point at which you should divert to that shelter rather than pressing on with the original plan.

Also define a man overboard protocol and practise it with the whole crew before departure. Not on paper: in the water, with the boat moving. Every minute of delay in a real recovery can be critical.

«The best contingency plan is the one you rehearsed on land before you needed it at sea.»

A summary to take on board

Planning checklist · Sailing voyage
  • Voyage type defined (coastal / offshore / ocean)
  • Route studied on official nautical chart, with anchorages and shelter marked
  • Distances, times and fuel consumption calculated
  • Weather forecast reviewed, departure window confirmed
  • Vessel inspected: hull, rig, engine, electrical systems
  • Safety equipment complete and validity verified
  • Crew organised with watch system in place
  • Documentation in order and zarpe completed at Port Captain's office
  • Contingency plan for each leg, with shelter port identified
  • Man overboard protocol practised with all crew

Good planning doesn't guarantee everything will go perfectly. The sea always has a surprise in store. But it does guarantee that when that surprise arrives, you'll be in a position to handle it.
