How to Sail the Canals of Southern Chile

What I learned over decades sailing those waters — and what nobody tells you before you set sail.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso Peirano  ·  June 2026  ·  12 min read

The canals of southern Chile are, without question, one of the most impressive places on the planet to sail. I say this from experience: I have sailed them in every possible condition, in different types of vessels, over decades. I've witnessed sunsets that leave you speechless, humpback whales just meters from the bow, passes so narrow the ship seems to touch both shores at once. And I have also lived the moments when those same waters turn serious and remind you that the sea is not a theme park.

This guide is not a tourist list. It's what I, as a Captain, would tell anyone who wants to sail southern Chile safely and make the most of it.

"Southern Chile does not forgive improvisation. But if you arrive prepared, it will give you experiences you won't find anywhere else in the world."

What are the austral canals?

Chile's austral canals are a labyrinth of interior waterways stretching from Puerto Montt, in the Los Lagos Region, to Cape Horn, the southernmost point of the American continent. More than 2,000 kilometers of protected routes formed by islands, peninsulas and fjords, making one of the most complex and beautiful inland water systems on the planet.

Geographically, the area divides into several sections: the northern Patagonia canals (between Puerto Montt and Chiloé Island), the southern Patagonia canals (from Chiloé to the Strait of Magellan), and the far south including the Beagle Channel and Cape Horn waters. Each section has its own character, conditions and challenges.

What makes this system special is that much of the navigation happens in protected waters — not open ocean — making it accessible to relatively small vessels and crews without offshore experience, as long as you plan well.

The truth about southern weather

The weather of southern Chile is the factor most underestimated by first-timers. It's not unmanageable — I've sailed those waters hundreds of times without major incidents — but you need to understand it well before you set sail.

In Patagonia, wind is the main character. The westerlies, known as the "roaring forties," arrive from the South Pacific loaded with energy and with nothing to stop them. In the canals, they're partially blocked by the geography, but in open passes or at the mouths of fjords, they can reach considerable force in a matter of minutes.

What you need to know about weather before setting sail
  • Conditions can change radically in 30 minutes — what was calm can become 30-knot winds
  • Rain is nearly constant year-round. Don't plan by the weather — plan by the weather windows
  • In summer (December–February) conditions are better, but not consistently good
  • Chilean Navy forecasts are more accurate for the area than international models
  • Windy and Passage Weather are useful, but always complement with the official met report
  • Mornings are generally calmer than afternoons — plan the most demanding passes for the morning

The best time to sail

Peak season is December to March. Daylight hours are longer (in the far south there are up to 18 hours of light in midsummer), temperatures are more tolerable and good weather windows are more frequent. If you can choose, that's the time.

That doesn't mean other seasons are impossible. I've sailed the canals in winter and it has been beautiful — snow on the hills, absolute silence, very few other vessels. But it demands more experience, more equipment and much higher tolerance for uncertainty.

Route planning: where to start

This is the most important part, and what most people get wrong. Planning a route in the southern canals is not the same as planning a Mediterranean coastal trip. The factors here are different.

Nautical charts

Use the SHOA charts (Chilean Navy Hydrographic and Oceanographic Service). They are the most accurate for these waters and are publicly accessible. Don't rely solely on third-party apps — they are a complement, not the primary tool.

Anchorages

In the austral canals you won't find marinas at every port. Most of the time you'll anchor in natural coves, sheltered bays, or tied to shore with anchor and lines. Knowing the available anchorages on each leg is critical — sometimes the difference between a safe and a dangerous anchorage is just a few hundred meters.

"Never set sail without knowing exactly where you'll sleep that night. In the south, improvising an anchorage can be very costly."

Narrow passes

Several canal passes have tide and current conditions that require crossing them at very specific times. Canal Chacao, the southern entrance to Chiloé, is the best-known example: currents can exceed 8 knots at the wrong hour, making forward progress impossible and even making the vessel difficult to steer.

The passes of the Strait of Magellan, further south, have their own logic. You need to study them carefully, calculate the tides and plan the crossing with margin.

Realistic timing

One of the most common mistakes: underestimating transit times. The canals are not highways. Some miles are covered at 3–4 knots due to headwinds, passes that can only be crossed at certain times, and anchorages that require waiting for a weather window. Plan with double the time you think you need.

Essential equipment for the south

Sailing southern Chile with the same gear you use on the central coast is a serious mistake. Water temperatures in Patagonia range between 4°C and 10°C for most of the year. Falling overboard without the right equipment can be fatal within minutes.

Equipment that is non-negotiable in the south
  • Immersion suits for every crew member — not life jackets, suits
  • Registered and active EPIRB — in the more remote canals there is no VHF coverage
  • Personal PLB for each crew member
  • Certified and accessible life raft
  • Fixed and handheld VHF — channel 16 always open
  • Main anchor and backup anchor with chain and trip line
  • Long shore lines (minimum 60 meters) for tying to shore
  • Printed nautical charts — never only digital
  • Extra fuel — services are scarce and distant
  • Provisions for more days than planned — weather can delay you

The paperwork: zarpe and official anchoring

In Chile, every vessel must obtain a zarpe (departure clearance) from the Harbour Master before going out to sea. This also applies to foreign vessels. It is not optional — the Navy carries out checks in the canals and sailing without a zarpe can have serious consequences.

The zarpe is processed at the nearest Harbour Master's office. For southern routes, the most important checkpoints are Puerto Montt, Castro, Chaitén, Puerto Natales and Punta Arenas. Find out the specific procedure for your planned route.

Communications in the canals

Mobile phone coverage in the austral canals is practically non-existent once you move away from populated centers. Don't count on your cell phone for emergencies. Marine VHF is your primary means of communication — with the Navy, other vessels and ports.

For truly remote areas, a satellite communication system (Iridium, mobile Starlink) is an investment that can be perfectly justified. Not as a replacement for VHF, but as a complement.

"In the southern canals, communication is not an accessory. It's part of crew safety."

What the south will give you

After all of this — the preparation, the planning, the equipment — comes the reward. And it is enormous.

The austral canals of Chile are one of the last places on earth where you can spend entire days without seeing another vessel. Where dolphins swim alongside the bow as if it were the most natural thing in the world. Where snow-capped volcanoes are reflected in perfectly still waters at dawn. Where the silence is so deep it becomes physical.

I have sailed in Angola, in the Gulf of Mexico, in the Caribbean, in Brazil. I have been to extraordinary places. And yet, every time I return to southern Chile, I realize there is nothing like it. Those waters have something that cannot be explained — only lived.

But you have to go prepared. And if you need help to prepare, that's what I'm here for.
