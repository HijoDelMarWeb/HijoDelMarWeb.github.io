How to Buy a Sailboat in Chile

The market, the process, the paperwork — and the traps that cost foreign buyers money.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso  ·  July 2026  ·  16 min read

Chile is an increasingly popular place to buy a cruising sailboat. Prices are generally lower than Europe or the US for comparable vessels, the fleet in key marinas includes a good cross-section of offshore-capable boats, and buying here means you're already positioned to sail immediately into some of the best cruising waters on the planet. But the local market has characteristics that trip up foreign buyers — limited transparency, informal sales processes, and sellers who are sometimes very good at presenting problems as cosmetic.

I have helped clients inspect and evaluate dozens of vessels in Chile over the years. This guide covers what I have seen work, what I have seen go wrong, and what every foreign buyer should understand before committing to a purchase.

The Chilean used sailboat market: what to expect

The Chilean sailing fleet is concentrated in a handful of locations: Valparaíso and Viña del Mar (the largest concentration), Puerto Montt (gateway to the channels, many cruising boats), Concepción and Talcahuano, and to a lesser extent Iquique and Arica in the north. If you are specifically looking for a boat ready for Patagonian sailing, Puerto Montt is the most logical search area.

The market is small by international standards — at any given moment there are perhaps 80–150 sailboats listed for sale across all platforms. This means limited choice but also means motivated sellers, and prices are often negotiable by 10–20% from listed asking prices, especially for boats that have been on the market for more than three months.

Price reality: A seaworthy 35–40ft fibreglass cruising sailboat in Chile typically sells for USD 30,000–80,000 depending on age, brand, and condition. European brands (Jeanneau, Bénéteau, Bavaria) are common. American designs (Islander, Catalina, Pacific Seacraft) appear occasionally. Aluminium and steel offshore vessels are rare and command a premium. Do not expect to find a bargain that nobody else has noticed — if a price looks dramatically low, there is a reason.

Where to find boats for sale

The main channels for finding used sailboats in Chile:

  • Yapo.cl — Chile's dominant classifieds platform. Search for "velero" or "embarcación". Listings range from dinghies to offshore cruisers. Photos are often poor and descriptions minimal, which is both a risk and an opportunity.
  • Facebook groups — "Veleros de Chile" and "Compra Venta de Embarcaciones Chile" are the most active. Many private sales happen here before they reach any other platform.
  • Marina bulletin boards — Physical notices at Club de Yates Valparaíso, Club Náutico Laguna Verde, Club de Yates Los Colonos (Puerto Montt), and Club de Deportes Acuáticos in Concepción. Some of the best deals never go online.
  • Word of mouth — If you know anyone in the Chilean sailing community, ask. Captains and marina staff know who is thinking about selling before the listing appears anywhere.
  • Brokers — There are a small number of marine brokers operating in Chile, primarily in Valparaíso and Puerto Montt. Their listings are more accurately described and priced, but they typically represent the seller, not the buyer.

The inspection: what you must do before any offer

The single most important step in buying a used sailboat in Chile is getting the vessel out of the water for a proper inspection. This is non-negotiable. The fibreglass hulls common in the Chilean fleet are particularly susceptible to osmotic blistering — a condition where water penetrates the gelcoat and causes delamination of the fibreglass layers, which can range from cosmetic to structurally serious and is essentially invisible when the boat is afloat.

A boat that has spent years in a Chilean marina without proper antifouling maintenance, or that was previously in fresh water (Lake Llanquihue and other lakes have active sailing communities), will often have significant osmosis that the seller either does not know about or is not disclosing.

What a proper out-of-water inspection covers

  • Hull below the waterline — osmotic blisters, impact damage, repairs (especially around the keel), paint condition and history
  • Keel attachment — the most critical structural point on a ballasted sailboat. Look for cracking in the fibreglass at the keel join, rust staining, and any movement when force is applied. This is an area where Chilean boats frequently show problems after years of sailing in swell without inspection.
  • Through-hull fittings — quantity, condition, material, and whether the seacocks actually operate. Many older Chilean boats have failed or seized seacocks that are a flooding risk.
  • Rudder — delamination, bearing condition, any play in the stock
  • Propeller and shaft — condition, alignment, cutlass bearing wear
  • Standing rigging — swaged terminals are the weak point. They can look clean externally while corroding internally at the swage. Any rigging over 10 years old should be replaced regardless of appearance.
  • Running rigging — UV degradation is severe in Chilean sun, especially in the north. Lines that look functional may have lost 50% of their working load limit.
  • Sails — condition, UV damage to leech tape, batten pockets, clew reinforcements. Getting sails inspected by a sailmaker is worthwhile for any significant purchase.
  • Engine — hours, service history, raw water impeller condition, heat exchanger, fuel system, mounts
  • Electrical system — Chilean boats often have decades of improvised wiring. Look for corroded connections, undersized wire, and non-marine-grade components.
  • Safety equipment — check actual expiry dates on flares, EPIRB battery date, life raft certification date. Much of what is presented as "safety equipment included" is expired and legally worthless.

Do not make an offer before the out-of-water inspection. A motivated seller will allow a haul-out as a condition of purchase. If a seller refuses to allow the boat to be lifted, that refusal tells you something important. The cost of haul-out and relaunch (typically USD 200–500 depending on the yard) is either shared by agreement or absorbed by the buyer — it is a small investment against the risk of a major structural surprise.

Getting a professional survey

Beyond your own visual inspection, commissioning a professional nautical survey from a qualified surveyor is the most reliable way to establish the true condition and value of a vessel before purchase. A survey provides a written report documenting every system, its condition, and any deficiencies — which gives you leverage in price negotiation and protects you legally if problems emerge after purchase.

In Chile, there are a limited number of qualified marine surveyors. An alternative used by many buyers is engaging a local captain with genuine technical knowledge to accompany the inspection — someone who has worked on similar vessels and knows what to look for in the specific context of Chilean sailing conditions.

A survey typically costs 1–3% of the vessel's estimated value and takes half a day to a full day depending on boat size and complexity. It is money almost always recovered in the negotiation, and occasionally it saves a buyer from a catastrophic mistake.

The paperwork: registration and legal process

Vessel registration in Chile is managed by Directemar — the Dirección General del Territorio Marítimo y de Marina Mercante, which falls under the Armada de Chile. Every vessel in Chilean waters must be registered, and the registration process for a change of ownership involves specific steps that take time.

What foreigners need to know

Foreigners can purchase and register a vessel in Chile. The process requires:

  • Chilean RUT (tax ID) — any foreigner with a valid passport can obtain a RUT at the Servicio de Impuestos Internos (SII). This is a straightforward process and takes about a week.
  • Purchase contract (contrato de compraventa) — must be a written document signed by both parties, ideally notarised. A verbal agreement has no legal standing.
  • Certificate of registration (Certificado de Matrícula) — issued by Directemar upon completion of the ownership transfer. The process varies by port but typically takes 2–6 weeks.
  • Certificate of nationality — if you are registering under Chilean flag, Directemar issues this document, which is what you present to Capitanías when requesting Zarpes.
  • Taxes — vessel purchase is subject to IVA (19% VAT) on the sale price if the seller is a company. Private sales between individuals are generally not VAT-liable, but confirm this with a local accountant.

Practical tip: The administrative process at Directemar is conducted in Spanish and assumes familiarity with Chilean maritime bureaucracy. Hiring a tramitador (a professional who handles paperwork on your behalf) for the registration process saves significant time and reduces errors. Most marina administrations can recommend one.

Importing a foreign-flagged vessel into Chile

If you are bringing a boat already registered in another country into Chilean waters for the purpose of sailing rather than purchasing locally, the process is different — you operate under a temporary import permit (importación temporal) which is managed through Aduana (customs). This permits the boat to remain in Chilean waters for a defined period and may be renewed. Consult with a customs agent in Valparaíso or Puerto Montt for current requirements, as the rules are periodically updated.

Costs beyond the purchase price

The purchase price of a sailboat in Chile is rarely the total cost of acquiring a sailing-ready vessel. Budget realistically for the following:

Item Typical range (USD) Notes
Pre-purchase survey / inspection 300 – 1,500 Depending on boat size and scope
Haul-out and relaunch 200 – 600 Per lift at Chilean yards
Antifouling paint and application 400 – 1,200 If the hull needs treatment
Standing rigging replacement 1,500 – 6,000 If wire is over 10 years or corroded
Safety equipment update 500 – 3,000 Life raft service, EPIRB, flares, suits
Engine service 200 – 800 Impeller, belts, filters, zincs
Registration transfer (Directemar) 150 – 400 Including tramitador fees
Marina fees while preparing 300 – 700 / month Varies significantly by port

A common mistake is buying a boat at a price that seems good, then discovering that bringing it to sailing-ready condition costs more than the savings. Build a realistic refit budget before you make an offer, not after.

The most common traps for foreign buyers

In twenty years of watching transactions in the Chilean sailing market, these are the patterns that recur most often:

"The engine was just serviced"

Fresh oil in the crankcase and a clean bilge are easy to fake. Ask for the service receipts. If they cannot be produced, assume the engine has not been serviced recently, budget accordingly, and have the engine inspected by a marine mechanic independently before closing.

"The rigging is fine, we sailed it last month"

Age matters more than recent use for standing rigging. Swaged wire terminals that are more than 10 years old are a risk regardless of how the boat looks or how recently it was sailed. Replace them — it is not negotiable for offshore sailing.

Safety equipment "included"

Life raft, EPIRB, flares, immersion suits — always check the actual certification and expiry dates. In many cases the "included" safety equipment is years out of date and legally void. Factor the replacement cost into your offer.

The verbal price reduction

Verbal agreements to reduce the price after problems are found in inspection are not enforceable in Chile. Get every negotiated change in writing, signed by the seller, before any money changes hands.

Rushed timelines

"I have another interested buyer" is a standard pressure tactic in any used goods market. A genuine seller of a legitimate boat will allow you reasonable time to conduct a proper inspection. If a seller is creating extreme urgency around a transaction, slow down rather than speed up.
