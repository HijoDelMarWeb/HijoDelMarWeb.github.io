Marine Radio Worldwide

Equipment, identification and certificates that apply in any country: what is common under the IMO and ITU, and what depends on your flag.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso · Updated November 2026 · 10 min read

Marine radio rules are not the same in every country. There is a common core set by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), and on top of it each country decides what it requires of its ships, its operators and its recreational boats. This guide explains what is common worldwide, what depends on your flag and on the countries you visit, and what to have in order before you cross a border. For Chile-specific requirements, see the guide to radio requirements in Chile.

The essentials in three lines:

  • The IMO requires GMDSS equipment on passenger ships and cargo ships of 300 GT and above on international voyages. A recreational sailboat is normally outside that regime.
  • What your boat carries is set mainly by the flag State, and the countries you visit may require more while you are in their waters.
  • Whatever the country, these five things are your base: a DSC VHF, an MMSI identity, a registered 406 MHz beacon, an operator certificate and a station licence where your country requires one.

Who the SOLAS convention binds

According to the IMO, under the GMDSS (Global Maritime Distress and Safety System) all passenger ships and all cargo ships over 300 gross tonnage on international voyages must carry specified terrestrial and satellite radiocommunications equipment to send and receive distress alerts, maritime safety information and general communications. The rules are in chapter IV of SOLAS.

Recreational boats that do not carry commercial passengers or cargo are normally outside SOLAS, so chapter IV does not bind them. That does not mean they have no rules: each flag State sets them, and many adopt simplified versions of the same scheme. A charter or commercial-use sailboat may fall under stricter national codes: confirm with your flag.

Sea areas A1 to A4

The GMDSS divides the sea into four areas according to communications coverage. What equipment a SOLAS ship needs depends on the areas it sails in, and many States use the same scheme as a guide for recreational boats.

A1Within radiotelephone coverage of at least one VHF coast station, with continuous DSC alerting available.VHF with DSC (channel 70)
A2Excludes A1. Within coverage of at least one MF coast station, with continuous DSC alerting available.MF with DSC (2,187.5 kHz)
A3Excludes A1 and A2. Since the SOLAS amendments in force from 1 January 2024, it is defined by the coverage of a recognised mobile satellite service, no longer only INMARSAT.Recognised satellite service

2024 changes. IMO resolution MSC.496(105) replaced the full text of chapter IV to modernise the GMDSS and entered into force on 1 January 2024. According to notes from classification societies and administrations, MF/HF equipment stopped being primary equipment in area A3 but remains so in A4. Administrations still read some details differently, so check your flag's current circular.

Identification: MMSI, call sign and station licence

MMSI. This is a nine-digit number that identifies your boat on DSC, AIS and beacons. Its format and use are regulated in article 19 of the ITU Radio Regulations and in ITU-R Recommendation M.585. It is assigned by your country's administration, and you need one before using a DSC radio. The first three digits (MID) indicate the country.

Ship station licence and call sign. Issued by the flag State. Some countries exempt recreational boats from the licence for local sailing but require it for international voyages; in the United States, for example, a recreational boat that uses only VHF and a beacon is "licensed by rule" for domestic waters and must apply for a licence if it makes international voyages. Every country has its own rule: confirm yours.

If you change owner, name or flag, the MMSI, the licence and the beacon registration must be updated. In many countries the licence and identities do not transfer and must be applied for again.

Operator certificates

There are two worlds of certificates. For SOLAS ships, the STCW convention (section A-IV/2) defines the General Operator's Certificate (GOC), valid in all areas, and the Restricted Operator's Certificate (ROC), valid only in area A1. For recreational boats, most countries use their own certificates, such as the Short Range Certificate (SRC) for VHF and the Long Range Certificate (LRC) for MF/HF and satellite, which are not part of the STCW regime. ITU Resolution 343 (WRC-97) and CEPT Recommendation T/R 31-03 harmonised the GOC and ROC examination syllabi, and the resolution seeks mutual recognition between administrations; in practice, confirm with each country whether it accepts your certificate.

One point that surprises people: in some countries, even if the boat's station is exempt from licensing, the operator must still hold the certificate that matches the equipment used.

406 MHz beacon registration

An unregistered beacon is far less useful: rescuers do not know whose it is or whom to call. According to Cospas-Sarsat, an International Beacon Registration Database (IBRD) exists, but it is intended only for countries without their own registry, and it does not replace the obligation of administrations, set by the IMO and ICAO, to provide a national registry. That is why you register in your flag country: the United Kingdom, for example, requires 406 MHz EPIRBs and PLBs on UK-flagged vessels to be registered in its national registry, and the United States requires registering them with NOAA before installation. Update the registration when you sell the boat, change the owner or change the flag.

Radio watch

On SOLAS ships the continuous watch is kept on DSC channel 70. The mandatory voice watch on channel 16 on GMDSS ships was kept until 1 February 2005, according to IMO resolutions. On recreational boats, the rule depends on the country: several authorities still ask for a channel 16 watch while the VHF is on and not being used to communicate. Follow your flag's rule and that of the country where you are.

Checklist before crossing a border

  • Fixed DSC VHF connected to GPS with the MMSI programmed. Check the programming.
  • Handheld VHF in a waterproof case with charged batteries.
  • 406 MHz beacon registered to your name and boat, with battery and expiry date up to date, and your MMSI or identity programmed.
  • A valid ship station licence, if your flag requires it for international voyages.
  • Operator certificate (SRC, LRC or equivalent) for whoever operates the radio, and confirmation that the country you are visiting accepts it.
  • For areas far from the coast: satellite communication or MF/HF, and the telephone numbers of the region's rescue coordination centres (MRCC).
  • Ask the maritime authority of the destination country whether it requires its own equipment, certificates or formalities for foreign boats.

Frequently asked questions

Is my recreational sailboat required to comply with SOLAS?

Normally not. SOLAS chapter IV binds passenger ships and cargo ships of 300 GT and above on international voyages. Recreational boats follow the rules of their flag State, which are often inspired by the same scheme. If the boat does charter or commercial carriage, it may fall under stricter national codes.

Do I need an MMSI?

If you use a DSC VHF, a beacon that encodes it or an AIS, yes. It is assigned by your country's administration and must be programmed into all the equipment.

Is my operator certificate valid in other countries?

It depends. The ITU promotes mutual recognition of the harmonised certificates, but each country decides. Before sailing in another country's waters, confirm with its maritime authority whether it accepts your certificate.

Where do I register my beacon?

In the national registry of your flag country. The Cospas-Sarsat international database is only for countries that do not have their own registry.

Which GMDSS sea area applies to my route?

The area depends on the real coverage of coast and satellite stations. Near populated coasts it is usually A1 (VHF); farther out, A2 or A3. Each country's maritime authority publishes the extent of its areas.
