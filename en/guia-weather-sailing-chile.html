Weather for Sailors in Chile: Sources, Reading Forecasts and When Not to Sail

The right weather source at the right time can make the difference between a great passage and a dangerous one. Here is what experienced sailors in Chile use.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso · July 2026 · 9 min read

I've seen a good number of maritime incidents that started with the phrase "according to Windy there was no problem." Windy is an excellent tool — I use it myself — but no numerical model replaces knowledge of the terrain, accumulated experience, and the ability to read signals the models don't capture. Marine weather reading is a skill, not just the act of checking a screen.

Why Marine Weather Is Different

Weather forecasting for sailors has different requirements than a general forecast. For a hike, what matters is whether it rains. For sailing, what matters is wind speed in knots, direction, the interval between gusts, sea state (wave height and period), visibility, and how conditions will change over the next 12-24 hours. A forecast that looks like "a bit of wind" on land can mean genuinely difficult conditions on the water.

Chile's geography also creates microclimates and local phenomena that global numerical models don't capture accurately. A boat in the Chacao Channel, in a Patagonian fjord, or in Quintero bay sits in a completely different micro-environment than the regional forecast suggests.

Weather Information Sources in Chile

SHOA — the official reference

The Servicio Hidrográfico y Oceanográfico de la Armada de Chile (shoa.cl) publishes marine forecasts, swell bulletins, storm warnings, and current and tide data. For the southern zone and the channels, it's the most reliable source because it's based on models adjusted for Chilean geography and observations from Navy stations. The Navy also broadcasts weather reports over VHF on a schedule — check with your local Port Captain for the channel and time for your zone.

Windy

Windy (windy.com) is the most useful visualisation tool for recreational sailors. It lets you view wind, swell and pressure fields across several models (ECMWF, GFS, ICON) and compare forecasts. It's especially good for coastal areas with enough observation density. Its main limitation is that low-resolution models don't capture local effects like the Puelche, channel willywaws, or wind acceleration through narrow passes.

PredictWind

PredictWind offers higher-resolution models and route forecasting for offshore sailing. It's the reference tool for planning long voyages, with route optimisation features based on forecast wind. It carries a subscription cost but it's worth it for anyone sailing offshore regularly.

How to Read a Wind Forecast

Wind is measured in knots (kt) and described by mean speed and gusts. The Beaufort scale is the practical reference for sailors:

Beaufort scale for sailors
  • Force 1-2 (1-6 kt): Calm to light air. Ideal conditions for beginners
  • Force 3 (7-10 kt): Gentle breeze. Good sailing wind, comfortable conditions
  • Force 4 (11-16 kt): Moderate breeze. Active conditions, a well-balanced boat sails with pleasure
  • Force 5 (17-21 kt): Fresh breeze. The limit for many recreational sailors. 1-2 m waves. The boat demands attention
  • Force 6 (22-27 kt): Strong breeze. Only with experience and a well-prepared vessel
  • Force 7+ (28+ kt): Near gale to storm. Don't sail; if you're already out, seek shelter

Local Phenomena the Models Don't Capture

The Puelche

A katabatic wind that descends from the Andes toward the Pacific, mainly between Valdivia and Chiloé. It forms when a mass of warm, dry air crosses the mountains and accelerates down the western slope via the Foehn effect. It can arrive suddenly with gusts of 50-80 knots. Global models don't predict it well because it operates at a sub-regional scale. Local sailors recognise it by visual signs: a clear sky to the east, dropping humidity, arc-shaped clouds over the mountains.

Willywaws in the southern channels

Willywaws (katabatic gusts) are downdraughts from the mountains that can exceed 60 knots in seconds, with no warning and from any direction. They don't appear on any forecast model because they're extremely local events. In Patagonia, they're a normal part of the sailing environment. The only real defence is correct anchoring (two anchors, a shore line) and knowing which anchorages are protected from which sectors.

When not to sail

Don't sail when the barometer is dropping more than 3 hPa per hour, when there's a swell warning for your zone, when the forecast is contradictory between models, when conditions exceed what your vessel and crew handle comfortably, or when you have doubts. The sea will still be there tomorrow.

Barometric Pressure as a Tool

The barometer is the most valuable instrument on board after the GPS. The practical rule: stable pressure above 1015 hPa indicates favourable conditions. Pressure below 1000 hPa indicates a depression. But what matters most is the change: a drop of more than 3 hPa in an hour indicates rapid deterioration. If you're sailing and the barometer drops that much in an hour, seek shelter before conditions worsen.
