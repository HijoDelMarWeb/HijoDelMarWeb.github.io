Hull Osmosis & Vacuum Drying

What the survey doesn't always tell you — and how to decide what to do with it.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso  ·  June 2026  ·  12 min read

Osmosis is the word that makes buyers go quiet during a survey. The surveyor taps the hull below the waterline, you hear a hollow ring instead of a solid thud, and suddenly the deal you were excited about is under a cloud. What happens next — whether you walk away, renegotiate, or proceed — depends almost entirely on understanding what osmosis actually is, how far it has progressed, and what it costs to fix.

I've inspected dozens of fiberglass hulls in Chilean marinas and boatyards over the years. Osmosis is present to some degree in the majority of fiberglass boats that have spent years in the water without barrier coat maintenance. That doesn't make it irrelevant — but it also doesn't make every osmosis diagnosis a catastrophe. The key is knowing the difference.

What osmosis is (and what it isn't)

Osmosis in a fiberglass hull is a process, not an event. Water molecules penetrate the gelcoat through microscopic pathways in the laminate and react with hydrophilic compounds inside the structure — unreacted resins, residual styrene, glass fiber sizing. This reaction creates a dilute acidic solution inside the laminate layers, which exerts osmotic pressure drawing in more water and gradually forming blisters visible below the waterline.

In its early stages, osmosis is a cosmetic-to-mild structural condition. In advanced stages — when widespread delamination has compromised the structural laminate or when the keel joint area is affected — it becomes a serious problem that can exceed the cost of the boat to repair.

The key distinction: osmosis confined to the gelcoat layer and the outer layers of laminate is a maintenance issue. Osmosis that has penetrated deep into the structural laminate — particularly in high-stress areas like the keel joint, chainplates and rudder — is a structural issue. The moisture meter reading tells you the first part of the story; only a professional inspection of the laminate cross-section tells you the second.

The inspection tools: what each one actually tells you

Tramex Skipper 5
The gold standard for moisture measurement in fiberglass. Non-destructive, calibrated for GRP (fiberglass). Gives readings across the hull surface in minutes. Reads in % moisture content. The most important instrument in any pre-purchase hull assessment.
Hammer Tap Test
Low-tech but revealing. Tap the hull systematically below the waterline and listen. A solid composite sounds crisp. A delaminated area sounds hollow. Identifies areas of delamination the moisture meter may not flag in its surface reading.
Infrared Camera
Detects temperature differential between wet and dry laminate. Useful for identifying moisture concentration zones that are not yet visible as blisters. Best used in controlled conditions — direct sun on the hull skews the reading.
Visual Inspection
The first step and never optional. Look for blister fields below the waterline — from pinhead size to coin-diameter. Count and map them. A boat with 20 small blisters is a different situation from one with 200 large blisters covering 60% of the wetted surface.
Core Sample
Destructive but definitive. A small plug removed from the hull cross-section shows exactly which layers are affected — gelcoat only, outer laminate, core material, or deep structural laminate. Requested in specialist surveys when the moisture reading and visual inspection are inconclusive.
Ultrasonic Gauge
Measures laminate thickness from one side of the hull only. Identifies areas where the laminate has thinned due to delamination or damage without requiring access from inside. Used to verify structural integrity in key areas.

Reading the moisture meter: what the numbers mean

Moisture meter readings alone don't tell you whether to buy or walk away — they tell you what questions to ask next. Here is a practical interpretation guide based on Tramex Skipper 5 readings on a dry fiberglass hull (not fresh out of the water — the hull should have been out for at least 48 hours):

Reading Interpretation Recommended Action
1 – 3% Dry laminate. Normal for a well-maintained hull with good barrier coat. Proceed. Inspect visually for surface blisters and request survey confirmation.
4 – 10% Elevated moisture. Early osmosis likely. May not be visible as blisters yet. Investigate further. Map the wet zones. Get a professional survey and cost estimate for barrier treatment. Use this as leverage in price negotiation.
10 – 20% Significant osmosis. Blister fields likely present. Some laminate delamination possible. Negotiate a meaningful price reduction that covers the full treatment cost plus haul-out. Request a core sample or specialist survey before committing. Don't proceed without professional assessment.
20%+ Severe osmosis. Structural delamination likely. Keel joint may be compromised. Red flag. Walk away unless the price is dramatically reduced and a specialist confirms the structural laminate is recoverable. This is repair territory that can easily exceed boat value.

The hull must be dry. Moisture meter readings taken immediately after haul-out are unreliable — the outer laminate is temporarily saturated. Wait at least 48 hours after lifting for a meaningful reading. Sellers who push for an immediate inspection right after haul-out and then argue the reading is "just surface water" should be viewed with appropriate suspicion.

The vacuum drying process: what actually happens

Vacuum drying is the professional standard for osmosis treatment. It is more thorough than simply letting a hull air-dry in the open, because it creates a controlled low-humidity environment that actively draws moisture from the laminate rather than waiting for passive evaporation. Here is what the process involves:

  1. 01
    Full Hull Stripping
    All antifouling paint is removed. Then the gelcoat is removed — typically by mechanical stripping or controlled grinding. In moderate to severe cases, the outer layers of contaminated laminate are also removed. The goal is to expose the affected material to the drying environment.
  2. 02
    Initial Moisture Mapping
    With the hull stripped, a systematic moisture map is created across the entire wetted surface. This establishes the baseline and identifies the areas of greatest concentration — usually the bow sections and areas around keel attachment points.
  3. 03
    Vacuum Tent Installation
    A sealed enclosure is built around the hull (or the hull is moved into a controlled environment). Dehumidifiers run continuously to maintain low relative humidity inside the enclosure. Some systems apply mild heat to accelerate moisture migration from the laminate.
  4. 04
    Monitored Drying Period
    This is the longest phase. Depending on initial moisture levels and laminate construction, drying takes 3 to 12 months. The laminate is checked periodically with the moisture meter. The process cannot be rushed without compromising the result.
  5. 05
    Final Moisture Verification
    Once readings consistently reach target levels (under 3%), a final comprehensive moisture map is conducted and documented. This is the technical baseline the hull will be held to going forward — and it should be included in any warranty offered by the yard.
  6. 06
    Re-Lamination and Barrier Coat
    Any removed laminate is replaced with epoxy laminate (not polyester — the epoxy system is what provides the osmosis resistance). A multi-coat epoxy barrier system (typically 5–7 coats) is applied over the entire wetted surface before new antifouling. Done correctly, this should last decades without osmosis recurrence.

Go / No-Go: a practical decision framework

Condition
Recommended Decision
Moisture 1–3%, no blisters, no hollow areas on tap test
✓ Go — proceed with full survey
Moisture 4–10%, some blistering, no structural delamination
⬦ Negotiate — price reduction to cover barrier treatment
Moisture 10–20%, blister fields, isolated hollow areas on tap test
⬦ Conditional — specialist survey + written quote for full treatment required before committing
Moisture 20%+, widespread delamination, keel area compromised
✗ Walk away unless price reflects full structural repair cost
Any level, but hull hasn't been out of water for 48+ hours at time of inspection
✗ Reschedule — reading is unreliable. Don't negotiate or commit based on it.

Use these figures before you make an offer. If you're considering a boat with moderate osmosis, get a written quote from a Chilean boatyard before you negotiate the purchase price — not after. The quote gives you hard numbers to anchor the negotiation, and protects you from discovering the treatment costs twice what you estimated once the boat is already yours.
