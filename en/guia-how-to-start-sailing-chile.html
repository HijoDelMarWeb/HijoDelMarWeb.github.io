How to Start Sailing in Chile

What you need to know before your first time aboard a sailboat — without spending on what you don't need yet.

By Captain Rodrigo Troncoso Peirano  ·  September 2026  ·  10 min read

Every so often someone writes to me with the same question, phrased in different ways: "I want to learn to sail, where do I start?". Almost always, they assume the first step is buying a sailboat or getting a licence. In thirty years at sea, I've seen that order is exactly backwards — and starting that way is the fastest route to spending money before you even know whether you enjoy sailing.

This guide is the path I'd recommend to anyone asking me today: no rush, no unnecessary spending, and steps you can take this very season.

Do You Need a Licence to Start?

No. This is the first surprise for most people. If you sail as crew or a guest aboard someone else's sailboat — which is how nearly everyone starts — you don't need any kind of nautical licence. A licence is only required for whoever is in command of the vessel.

That means you can spend your entire first season on the water, learning to trim sails, read the wind, and understand basic manoeuvres, without having filed anything with the maritime authority. The licence comes later, once you decide you want to be the one at the helm independently.

When you do need a licence
  • When you'll be the skipper (in command) of a vessel
  • When you buy your own sailboat and sail without another skipper aboard
  • The type of licence depends on vessel size and navigation category — see the nautical licence guide below
  • You don't need one to sail as crew, a guest, or a sailing school student

The Fastest Path: Don't Buy a Boat Yet

The most common mistake I see is someone who decides they like the idea of sailing and, having set foot on a sailboat only a couple of times, starts shopping for boats to buy. It's like learning to drive by buying a race car first.

The path that actually works is sailing as crew on other people's boats for a season — ideally several outings with different skippers, in different conditions. This gives you three things no rushed purchase can: real experience, a sense of what kind of sailing you enjoy (racing? cruising? southern channels?), and an honest read on how much time and money you're willing to commit.

"Nobody buys their first sailboat well the first time if they've never sailed someone else's boat before."

Yacht Clubs and Sailing Schools

Yacht clubs are, by far, the most effective entry point. Almost all of them run beginner sailing schools, seasonal activities, and — most valuably — an environment where skippers who need crew for races or weekend outings are constantly looking for new people.

Chile has active clubs in several areas: in the central zone, around Valparaíso and Algarrobo; in Santiago, at nearby reservoirs and lakes where dinghy sailing is common; and in the south, around Puerto Montt and the channel region, where the sailing culture is different and more cruising-oriented. Every club has its own personality — it's worth visiting more than one before deciding where to get involved.

Independent sailing schools are another option, especially if you prefer more structured learning with a certified instructor from day one. They typically offer weekend or full-week courses combining theory and practical sailing.

How to get your first outings
  • Join or visit nearby yacht clubs — many run open days for the public
  • Ask directly at the club whether skippers are looking for occasional crew
  • Weekend social races almost always need extra crew
  • Chilean sailing Facebook groups and communities regularly post opportunities to sail
  • Consider a short introductory course — it significantly speeds up the early learning curve

Beginner Zones vs. Advanced Zones

Not all of Chile's coastline is equally demanding. When starting out, it makes sense to sail in protected bays and relatively calm waters, where wind and current are more predictable and the margin for error is larger. The central zone — the bays around Valparaíso, Quintero, Algarrobo — serves that purpose well.

The southern channels and Patagonia are an entirely different story: katabatic winds, strong tides, and technical, demanding navigation even for sailors with years of experience. It's not a place to learn — it's a place you arrive at after accumulating real experience in simpler waters. If that region interests you down the road, it's worth first reading about what sailing in Chile generally involves and what Patagonia specifically demands.

An honest word of caution

I've seen enthusiastic newcomers buy a sailboat and plan their first voyage south almost immediately. It's a recipe for a bad time, or worse. Build up miles in protected waters first. The southern channels will still be there when you're ready.

Minimum Equipment You Should Understand

Even though as crew you're not responsible for the vessel's safety equipment, understanding what's aboard and what it's for is part of genuinely learning to sail. Life jackets, how the VHF radio works, where the first-aid kit is, how to activate the EPIRB if there is one — ask your skipper about these things on your first outings. A good skipper explains them without you having to ask.

Buy or Charter at the Start?

Neither, not yet. Chartering makes sense once you already know what type of sailboat you enjoy handling and want to practise independently before buying. Buying makes sense once you've sailed for a season or more and have clarity on the kind of sailing you want to do regularly.

Skipping the "sail with others" phase and jumping straight to buying is, in my experience, the decision that generates the most regret. It ends in boats poorly matched to how they're actually used, or in people who lose interest quickly because they discover — boat already purchased — that they didn't enjoy sailing as much as they thought.

What It Really Costs to Start

The good news: starting out is cheap compared to what most people imagine. Without buying a boat, typical costs are yacht club membership (variable by club), occasional introductory courses, and contributing to fuel or maintenance costs when sailing as occasional crew — usually somewhere between CLP 20,000 and 60,000 per day sail, depending on the vessel and distance.

A formal multi-day course with a certified sailing school can cost between CLP 150,000 and 400,000, depending on duration and whether it includes certification. Compared to the cost of buying and maintaining your own sailboat, it's a minimal investment to find out whether this is really for you.

Practical Advice from the Captain

If you asked me how I'd start today, with no prior experience: first, I'd join or become a regular visitor at a nearby yacht club. Second, I'd actively look for opportunities to sail as crew, even if it meant just folding sails or helping with simple manoeuvres at the beginning. Third, I'd take a short introductory course at some point during that first season — not before, so the theory makes sense against what you've already experienced on the water.

And fourth: don't rush the decision to buy. The right sailboat for you will be far clearer after a season sailing with others than after reading a hundred listings.
